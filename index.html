Case study 01

One version of the truth for retail sales

24 Power BI reports for every department, all fed from one semantic model and one agreed set of KPI definitions.

The problem

The business sells through 100+ POS branches, a web store, an app, marketplaces and wholesale arms. Each department reported "sales" differently. Some numbers included shipping fees and some didn't. Online orders shipped from a warehouse branch were missing from budget achievement.

What I did

  1. Agreed the KPI definitions with management before building anything. Shipping, cash-on-delivery and admin fees are pass-through charges, so they count as neither sales nor discounts. Product discounts and loyalty points do reduce net sales. Online orders shipped from the distribution warehouse count toward that branch's budget.
  2. Split sales into the channels the business actually manages: POS, Online, Marketplace, Direct, and separate wholesale and factory arms, assigned by sales team in a fixed order of priority.
  3. Built comparable-period analysis: last year, like-for-like (same-store) and target achievement, with daily targets by branch and category.
  4. Built a returns analysis by area, month and product. I found barcode scans typed as quantities, up to 500M+ units, that turned the returns total into nonsense, and capped them without changing net sales.
  5. Answered a new business question: are new branch managers holding their branches? Each branch is compared with the same period last year and with the network trend. Branches that didn't exist last year are compared with their own average since opening.

Impact

  • Every department reads the same number for the same question.
  • One model holds the full page set (35 pages), and departmental reports reuse it, so a fix is made once.
Case study 02

Cutting the daily data sync cost by 99% on the smallest Fabric capacity

The nightly ERP sync went from about 8,200 CU-seconds to about 50, and a trivial report query went from a flat 21 seconds to under 1 second.

The problem

The whole platform runs on a Microsoft Fabric F2, the smallest capacity: 2 compute units and 3 GB of memory per model. Upgrading was not an option. Every query took about 21 seconds whatever it did. That flat delay is Fabric throttling, not slow DAX.

How I diagnosed it

  • Used the Fabric Capacity Metrics app to see which item consumed what, hour by hour.
  • About 60% of all compute was the database staying awake. Anything that queried it kept it awake: the sync, DirectQuery visuals, Desktop refreshes.
  • The Spark sync billed a flat ~8,200 CU-s per run, and its time did not grow with the data. The cost was Spark overhead, not work.
  • Scripted validation queries cost 75 to 400 CU-s each, against 2 to 10 for a normal viewer.

Compute per sync run

Spark (before)
~8,200 CU-s
Python (after)
~50 CU-s

What I changed

  1. Rebuilt the sync as a plain Python notebook. I checked it against Spark on the same 24-hour window, table by table: 30 tables, 51,367 rows identical.
  2. Added retry with backoff, because the capacity accepts one ingest at a time.
  3. Moved tables from DirectQuery to Import (6 DirectQuery tables down to 1), so reports stopped waking the database.
  4. Moved automated report reads from the Power BI service to the database, and turned off Copilot on the capacity.
  5. Ran the sync once a day instead of several times.
Case study 03

Running the company's reporting cycle

32 scheduled and on-demand reports, from daily to quarterly, for teams across the business.

The problem

Every team needs its own view of the same data, on its own schedule. The job is to turn one dataset into the report each team acts on, on time, with numbers that agree across teams.

FrequencyReportsExamplesUsed by
Daily1Supervisors' daily achievementArea supervisors
Weekly2Supervisors' weekly achievement; branches below 80% of targetSupervisors, sales management
Twice a month3Customer registration rate; quality committee; influencer salesCustomer service, quality, marketing
Monthly15Branch stock, returns, category target achievement, key products, monthly offers, category deep dives, overtime, stocktakeManagement, category, inventory, HR
Quarterly4Branch and branch-manager achievement; brand and category sales vs stockManagement, purchasing

Where the value is

  • Exception reports, not just totals. The "below 80% of target" reports tell supervisors which branches and categories to act on first.
  • Sales tied to stock. Brand and category sales are compared with stock each quarter, so purchasing sees what sells and what sits.
  • Measuring impact. On-demand reports test whether a team's work, such as training or planogram changes, moved sales.

Next step: automation

Most of these reports are built by hand today. In September 2026 I automated the first two: a daily sales workbook that took 11 minutes by hand, and a monthly report of online products sold below cost. Both matched the manual version cell for cell before I switched over. The rest follow the same way, one report at a time.

Case study 04

Measuring online promotions the ERP never recorded

Online offer performance (bundles sold, value, profit, margin, invoices) is now reported like branch promotions, and the total reconciles exactly with net online sales.

The problem

In branches, the POS tags every sale with its promotion. Online orders carry no promotion tag at all, so management couldn't see which online bundles sold or whether they made money.

What I did

  1. Turned the business rule into logic: an offer counts as sold when every product of an active bundle is on the same invoice.
  2. Handled the edge cases. Bundles sold = the smallest multiple of the required quantities. A product in several matching bundles goes to the biggest one, so nothing is counted twice. Refunds come back as negative rows, so the parts add up exactly to net sales.
  3. Stated the limitation: "any N of these products" offers can't be detected from invoice lines, so they are left out and labelled.
